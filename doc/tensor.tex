\chapter{Tensors and Symmetries}
We will discuss the notions of tensors and symmetries in this chapter. We will mostly follow the conventions of \cite{tensorkit,tenpy2024}. 
\section{Linear Representations of Groups}
\begin{definition}
Let $G$ be a compact group. A \emph{linear representation} of $G$ on a finite dimensional vector space $\mathscr{V}$ is a homomorphism $\rho:G\mapsto\mathsf{GL}(\mathscr{V})$. We will assume the representation is \emph{unitary}, i.e. $\rho(g)^\dagger=\rho(g^{-1})$ for all $g\in G$. 
\end{definition}

It can be shown that any linear representation of a compact group decomposes into a direct sum of irreducible representations, $\rho_a$. We use latin letters $a,b,c,\ldots$ for the irreps, and call them sectors, charges or irreps interchangeably. The space on which $\rho_a$ acts is $\mathscr{V}^a$, of dimension $d_a$. A space carrying an arbitrary representation is written with a subscript, $\mathscr{V}_i$; its $a$-isotypic part is the subspace $\mathscr{V}_i^a\subset\mathscr{V}_i$, and $\mathscr{V}_i=\bigoplus_a m_i^a\mathscr{V}^a$, where the integer $m_i^a$ is the multiplicity, the number of copies of sector $a$ in $\mathscr{V}_i$. When only the sector matters we write $(a)$ for the space $\mathscr{V}^a$, and $m\cdot(a)$ for $m$ copies of it.

\begin{example}
Let \(G=\mathbb Z_n=\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\) be the cyclic group written additively.
Identify \(\mathbb C^n\) with
\[
\mathbb C^n=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb C}\{e_m:m\in\mathbb Z_n\},
\]
where \(e_m\) is the standard basis vector indexed by \(m\in\mathbb Z_n\).
Define
\[
\rho:\mathbb Z_n\to \mathrm{GL}(\mathbb C^n),
\qquad
\rho(p)e_m:=e_{m+p},
\]
with subscripts taken modulo \(n\). This is a unitary representation: \(\rho(p)\) is a permutation matrix.

Let
\[
\zeta_n:=e^{2\pi i/n}.
\]
For each \(a\in\mathbb Z_n\), define
\[
v_a:=\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z_n}\zeta_n^{-am}e_m,
\qquad
\mathscr{V}^a:=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb C}\{v_a\}=\mathbb C v_a.
\]
Then for every \(p\in\mathbb Z_n\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
\rho(p)v_a
&=\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z_n}\zeta_n^{-am}\rho(p)e_m
=\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z_n}\zeta_n^{-am}e_{m+p} \\
&=\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z_n}\zeta_n^{-a(j-p)}e_j
=\zeta_n^{ap}\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z_n}\zeta_n^{-aj}e_j
=\zeta_n^{ap}v_a.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus \(\mathscr{V}^a\) is invariant under \(\rho\), and \(\rho|_{\mathscr{V}^a}\) is the one-dimensional representation
\[
\rho_a(p)=\zeta_n^{ap}=e^{2\pi i ap/n}
\qquad (p\in\mathbb Z_n).
\]
The vectors \(\{v_a\}_{a\in\mathbb Z_n}\) form the Fourier basis of \(\mathbb C^n\), so
\[
\mathbb C^n=\bigoplus_{a\in\mathbb Z_n}\mathscr{V}^a,
\qquad
\rho\cong\bigoplus_{a\in\mathbb Z_n}\rho_a.
\]

If one keeps the opposite sign convention, define instead
\[
v_a=\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z_n}\zeta_n^{am}e_m,
\]
in which case the eigenvalue is
\[
\zeta_n^{-ap}=e^{-2\pi i ap/n}.
\]
Physically, this is the Fourier transform, which diagonalizes the action of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ on $\mathbb{C}^n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $G = \mathsf{SU}(2)$. Its Lie algebra is spanned by the angular momentum operators $\{J_x,J_y,J_z\}$, with $[J_i,J_j]=i\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k$. Its irreducible representations are labeled by a non-negative half-integer $a\in\{0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,\ldots\}$, and $d_a=2a+1$. The representation $\rho_{1/2}$ is the fundamental representation of $\mathsf{SU}(2)$, and $\rho_1$ is the adjoint representation. 
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
    Let $f:\mathscr{V}\to\mathscr{W}$ be a linear map between two vector spaces carrying representations $\rho_\mathscr{V}$ and $\rho_\mathscr{W}$ of a group $G$. We say that $f$ is an \emph{intertwiner} if it commutes with the action of $G$, i.e. $f\circ \rho_\mathscr{V}(g) = \rho_\mathscr{W}(g)\circ f$ for all $g\in G$.
\end{definition}
We will write $f\in\hom_G(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{W})$ if $f$ is an intertwiner between $\mathscr{V}$ and $\mathscr{W}$. 

From a categorical perspective, the vector spaces carrying representations of a group $G$ form a category $\mathsf{Rep}(G)$, whose objects are the vector spaces carrying representations and whose morphisms are the intertwiners. 

Finally, let us mention the following important result, which will be used in the following discussion.
\begin{theorem}[Schur's Lemma]
    Let $\mathscr{V}$ and $\mathscr{W}$ be two irreducible representations of a group $G$. If $f\in\hom_G(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{W})$, then either $f=0$ or $f$ is an isomorphism. In particular, if $\mathscr{V}=\mathscr{W}$, then $f = \lambda \mathrm{id}_\mathscr{V}$ for some $\lambda\in \mathbb{C}$.
\end{theorem}

In general we extend the notion of intertwiner. A linear map acts on $\hom(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{W})\simeq \mathscr{V}^*\otimes \mathscr{W}$ as\begin{align*}
    \rho(g)f = \rho_\mathscr{W}(g)\,f\,\rho_\mathscr{V}(g)^{-1},\quad \forall g\in G,
\end{align*}
using unitarity, $\rho_\mathscr{V}(g)^\dagger=\rho_\mathscr{V}(g)^{-1}$. Then $f$ is an intertwiner if and only if it is invariant under $G$, $\rho(g)f = f$ for all $g\in G$. Since $\mathscr{V}^*\otimes \mathscr{W}$ decomposes into irreps, a map can also transform in a nontrivial sector: we call $f$ an \emph{$a$-intertwiner} if it lies in the $a$-isotypic part,\begin{align*}
    \hom_a(\mathscr V,\mathscr W):=\Bigl\{f:\mathscr V\to\mathscr W\  \Bigm|\ \rho_{\mathscr
W}(g)\,f\,\rho_{\mathscr V}(g)^{-1}=\rho_a(g)\,f\ \ \forall g\in G\Bigr\},
\end{align*}
where $\rho_a$ is the representation of the irrep $a$.\footnote{The right hand side is shorthand: $\rho_a$ acts on the $a$-isotypic component of $\mathrm{Hom}(\mathscr V,\mathscr W)$, not on the target of $f$. The precise statement is $f\in(\mathscr V^*\otimes\mathscr W)_a$.} We have $\hom_G = \hom_0$, the trivial sector. The decomposition then implies\begin{align*}
    \hom(\mathscr V,\mathscr W) = \bigoplus_a \hom_a(\mathscr V,\mathscr W).
\end{align*}

The last ingredient is the tensor product, on which $G$ acts diagonally:\begin{align}
    \rho_{\mathscr{V}_1\otimes\mathscr{V}_2}(g) = \rho_{\mathscr{V}_1}(g)\otimes \rho_{\mathscr{V}_2}(g),\quad \forall g\in G,
\end{align}
and this extends to any number of factors. 

\begin{definition}
A tensor of rank $(r,s)$ is a multilinear map \[
    T:\underbrace{\mathscr{V}_1\times\cdots\times \mathscr{V}_r}_{= \mathscr{V}_{\mathrm{in}}}\to \mathscr{V}_{\mathrm{out}},\qquad \mathscr{V}_{\mathrm{out}}=\mathscr{V}_{r+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathscr{V}_{r+s},
\]
where each $\mathscr{V}_i$ carries a representation $\rho_i$ of a group $G$ (not necessarily irreducible), and $\mathscr{V}_{\mathrm{in}}=\mathscr{V}_1\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathscr{V}_r$. Multilinear means linear in each argument. Physically the $\mathscr{V}_i$ are local (physical or virtual) Hilbert spaces. 

We say a tensor $T$ carries charge $a$ if\begin{align}
    T\in\hom_a(\mathscr{V}_{\mathrm{in}},\mathscr{V}_{\mathrm{out}}).
\end{align}
If $T$ carries the trivial charge we say that $T$ is invariant under the action of $G$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
Let $G=\mathbb{Z}_2$. The irreps are labeled by $a\in\{0,1\}$, and $\rho_a$ acts on a one-dimensional space $\mathscr{V}^a$, with $\rho_a\otimes \rho_b = \rho_{(a+b)\mod 2}$. An invariant rank-2 tensor $T\in \hom_G(\mathscr{V}_1\otimes \mathscr{V}_2,\mathbb{C})$ can then be nonzero only on $\mathscr{V}_1^a\otimes \mathscr{V}_2^b$ with $(a+b)\mod 2=0$, i.e. $b=a$: it connects only sectors of the same charge. Concretely, for $v_1\in \mathscr{V}_1^{a}$ and $v_2\in \mathscr{V}_2^{b}$, invariance requires
\begin{align}
    T(v_1,v_2) = T(\rho_a(g)v_1,\rho_b(g)v_2) = T((-1)^{ag}v_1,(-1)^{bg}v_2) = (-1)^{(a+b)g}T(v_1,v_2),\quad \forall g\in \mathbb{Z}_2.
\end{align}
This implies that $T(v_1,v_2)=0$ unless $(a+b)\mod 2=0$.

On the other hand, if $T$ carries charge $c$, that is, it transforms as \begin{align}
    T(\rho_a(g)v_1,\rho_b(g)v_2) = \rho_c(g)T(v_1,v_2),\quad \forall g\in \mathbb{Z}_2,
\end{align}
then we must have $(a+b)\mod 2 = c$. This means that the invariant tensor can only connect sectors with charges that sum to $c$ modulo $2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $G=\mathsf{U}(1)$. The irreps are labeled by integers $a\in\mathbb{Z}$, and $\rho_a$ acts on a one-dimensional space $\mathscr{V}^a$, with $\rho_a\otimes \rho_b = \rho_{a+b}$. An invariant rank-2 tensor $T\in \hom_G(\mathscr{V}_1\otimes \mathscr{V}_2,\mathbb{C})$ can then be nonzero only on $\mathscr{V}_1^a\otimes \mathscr{V}_2^b$ with $a+b=0$, i.e. $b=-a$: it connects only sectors of opposite charge, which is charge conservation. Concretely, for $v_1\in \mathscr{V}_1^{a}$ and $v_2\in \mathscr{V}_2^{b}$, invariance requires
\begin{align}
    T(v_1,v_2) = T(\rho_a(g)v_1,\rho_b(g)v_2) = T(e^{iag}v_1,e^{ibg}v_2) = e^{i(a+b)g}T(v_1,v_2),\quad \forall g\in \mathsf{U}(1).
\end{align}
This implies that $T(v_1,v_2)=0$ unless $a+b=0$.
\end{example}


The two examples above are simple because $G$ is abelian and its irreps are one-dimensional. A non-abelian group has higher-dimensional irreps, and the tensor product of two irreps decomposes into a direct sum of several irreps. 

\begin{example}
Let $G=\mathsf{SU}(2)$ and let $T\in\hom_G(\mathscr{V}_1\otimes\mathscr{V}_2,\mathbb{C})$ be a $(2,0)$ invariant tensor, where\begin{align}
    \mathscr{V}_1 = \mathscr{V}^{0}\oplus\mathscr{V}^{1}\oplus\mathscr{V}^{2}\oplus\mathscr{V}^{3},\quad \mathscr{V}_2 = \mathscr{V}^{0}\oplus\mathscr{V}^{1}\oplus\mathscr{V}^{2}.
\end{align}Their tensor product distributes as

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
        $\mathscr{V}_2 \backslash \mathscr{V}_1$& $\mathscr{V}_1^{0}$ & $\mathscr{V}_1^{1}$ & $\mathscr{V}_1^{2}$ & $\mathscr{V}_1^{3}$ \\
        \hline
        $\mathscr{V}_2^{0}$ & $(0)$ & $(1)$ & $(2)$ & $(3)$ \\
        $\mathscr{V}_2^{1}$ & $(1)$ & $(0)\oplus(1)\oplus(2)$ & $(1)\oplus(2)\oplus(3)$ & $(2)\oplus(3)$ \\
        $\mathscr{V}_2^{2}$ & $(2)$ & $(1)\oplus(2)\oplus(3)$ & $(0)\oplus(1)\oplus(2)\oplus(3)\oplus(4)$ & $(1)\oplus(2)\oplus(3)$ 
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}
For $T$ to be invariant only the blocks $\mathscr{V}_1^{0}\otimes \mathscr{V}_2^{0}$, $\mathscr{V}_1^{1}\otimes \mathscr{V}_2^{1}$ and $\mathscr{V}_1^{2}\otimes \mathscr{V}_2^{2}$ can contribute; every other summand of the tensor product contains no copy of the trivial representation. In matrix language $T$ is the direct sum of maps $\mathscr{V}_1^{a}\to(\mathscr{V}_2^{a})^*$ for $a=0,1,2$.

The array is therefore block diagonal, one block per sector. (Every $\mathsf{SU}(2)$ irrep is self-dual, $\mathscr{V}_1^*\simeq\mathscr{V}_1$ and $\mathscr{V}_2^*\simeq\mathscr{V}_2$, which is why the block of sector $a$ sits on the diagonal.)


By Schur's lemma a map between equivalent irreps is a multiple of the identity, so each block is one number times the identity: a single scalar per sector.
\end{example}
\section{Fusion trees}
\label{sec:tree}
The last section left us with a block-diagonal tensor: a sequence of sectors $(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ fuses to the charge $c$ of the tensor. For two legs each block is a single number, by Schur's lemma. For three legs and higher the decomposition is finer: the blocks are no longer numbers, and describing them is the subject of this section.
\subsection{Clebsch--Gordan Tensor}
\label{sec:cg}

\begin{definition}
Let $a,b,c$ be sectors. $\mathscr{V}^{a}$ carries the irreducible representation $a$, of dimension $d_a$, with an orthonormal basis $\{\ket{\alpha}_a\}_{\alpha=1}^{d_a}$. The group $G$ acts on the tensor space $\mathscr{V}^{a}\otimes\mathscr{V}^{b}$ through $\rho_a(g)\otimes\rho_b(g)$. This is in general reducible:\footnote{The label $c$ also marks the copy: for $\mathsf{SU}(3)$, $8\otimes8$ contains $8$ twice, and its two copies are written $8$ and $8'$ and treated as different charges $c$.}
\begin{align}
    \mathscr{V}^{a}\otimes\mathscr{V}^{b}\cong\bigoplus_{c}\mathscr{V}^{c},
\end{align}
over the labelled copies $c$. The \emph{Clebsch--Gordan tensor} is the embedding \begin{align}
    C^c_{ab}:\mathscr{V}^c\hookrightarrow \mathscr{V}^a\otimes\mathscr{V}^b.
\end{align}
In components it is the inner product
\begin{align}
    (C^{c}_{ab})^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta} = \langle a\alpha ; b\beta\mid  c\mu\rangle,\label{eq:cg}
\end{align}
one entry for each triple of component labels that meet at a node.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
For an abelian group every $d_a=1$, every product contains each charge at most once, and the overlap is $1$ up to a phase of the convention. Recoupling is then trivial, and the whole content of this section reduces to the sparsity of the fusion rules that the previous section already used.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Rank 2} A rank-2 tensor fuses its two incoming legs into the charge it carries, and Eq.(\ref{eq:cg}) gives that fused state:\begin{align}
    \ket{c\mu} = \sum_{\alpha,\beta}(C^{c}_{ab})^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}\,\ket{\alpha}_a\otimes\ket{\beta}_b,
\end{align}
with the Clebsch--Gordan tensors orthonormal,
\begin{align}
    \sum_{\alpha,\beta}(C^{c}_{ab})^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}\,
    \overline{(C^{c'}_{ab})^{\mu'}_{\alpha\beta}}=\delta_{cc'}\delta_{\mu\mu'},
    \qquad
    \sum_{c,\mu}(C^{c}_{ab})^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}\,
    \overline{(C^{c}_{ab})^{\mu}_{\alpha'\beta'}}=\delta_{\alpha\alpha'}\delta_{\beta\beta'} . \label{eq:cg-orth}
\end{align}
where the former uses the resolution of identity on $\mathscr{V}^{a}\otimes\mathscr{V}^{b}$ and the latter on $\mathscr{V}^{c}$. The first line says that the map is an isometry, the second that it is a complete embedding. The two together say that the blocks tile a unitary matrix.

To conclude, thanks to Schur's lemma, we are able to view the fusion trees as vectors in a Hilbert space, for the same fusion process, the trees form a orthonormal (to see this, use the relation Eq.(\ref{eq:cg-orth})) basis labeled by the intermediate fusion step $b$. We can further define the inner product of two trees, and $F$-symbols are the overlaps of two different fusion ordering, and act as the change of basis between the two bases. 
\begin{example}
For $\mathsf{SU}(2)$, with $a_i=j_i$, $b=j_{12}$, $b'=j_{23}$ and $c=J$, the overlap is
\begin{align}
    (F^{J}_{j_1j_2j_3})_{j_{12}j_{23}}=(-1)^{j_1+j_2+j_3+J}\,\sqrt{d_{j_{12}}d_{j_{23}}}\;
    \begin{Bmatrix}j_1&j_2&j_{12}\\ j_3&J&j_{23}\end{Bmatrix},\label{eq:6j}
\end{align}
independent of $M$, and zero unless each of the three nodes satisfies its triangle condition. The sign is a convention. It comes from the order in which the two legs are written inside a coupled state, and a different convention multiplies each vector of a basis by a sign or a phase; only the combination of the conventions appears in an observable, which is the content of the identities of Section~\ref{sec:frandr}. The $6j$ symbols are tabulated and have their own orthogonality, which is the statement that the two bases are orthonormal.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}
The matrix $(F^{c}_{a_1a_2a_3})$ is unitary,
\begin{align}
    \sum_{b'}(F^{c}_{a_1a_2a_3})_{bb'}\,\overline{(F^{c}_{a_1a_2a_3})_{b''b'}}=\delta_{bb''}.
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
This says that the two tree bases are orthonormal: $F$ is the change of basis between them, so it is unitary.

\begin{example}
The smallest case with more than one inner label is three spin-$\tfrac12$ legs, where $b,b'\in\{0,1\}$. For $c=\tfrac12$, Eq.(\ref{eq:6j}) gives
\begin{align}
    F^{1/2}_{1/2,1/2,1/2}=\begin{pmatrix}
    -\tfrac12 & \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[2pt]
    \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} & \tfrac12
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{align}
with rows and columns ordered as $(b,b')=(0,1)$. The matrix is real, symmetric and of trace zero, so $F^2=\mathbf{1}$: its eigenvalues are $+1$ and $-1$, and $F=F^\dagger=F^{-1}$, which is unitarity in its cheapest form. For $c=\tfrac32$ only $b=b'=1$ is allowed and $F^{3/2}=(1)$. The two matrices together are the change of basis between the two bracketings of three spin-$\tfrac12$ states, and they are the smallest nontrivial example of the symbol.
\end{example}

\section{Blocks and their keys}
As we saw earlier, a tensor is a linear map between vector spaces, and a vector space is a direct sum of irreps. A tensor with charge $c$ on the root therefore splits into blocks, one per fusion tree that fuses the leg charges to $c$; inside a block the entries are labelled by the trees on the two sides of the map. Two fusion sequences give the same block, related by the unitary $F$ symbol, so we may fix one deterministic sequence per tree and store the coefficients in that tree basis. 

In our convention, we always store the tree as\begin{align}
    (a_1,\dots,a_r|b_1,\dots,b_{r-2}|c),
\end{align}
where the fusion process is from left to right.

\begin{example}
Let $G=\mathsf{SU}(2)$ and consider an invariant rank-3 tensor $T\in \hom_G(\mathscr{V}_1\otimes\mathscr{V}_2\otimes\mathscr{V}_3,\mathbb{C})$ with

The right one is the number of numbers stored, one per triple of multiplets. The symmetry thus compresses the tensor twice over. Sparsity removes the ten forbidden leg-charge combinations, $180000\to 78400$. The multiplet structure then removes the CG degeneracy, $78400\to 10600$. The result is $10600\times 16\ \mathrm{B}=169.6\ \mathrm{kB}$, a factor $17$ below the dense array. The multiplicities here are already ten times the smallest case $7,5,3,1$, and the ratio did not move. The saving is a fixed overhead of the group, not an exponential one.

Only two values of the first inner charge, $b_1=0$ and $b_1=1$, are consistent: two blocks, one per inner charge, built from different products of CG tensors even though the leaves are the same. 
\end{example}

\section{$F$, $R$ and Fusion Category}
\label{sec:frandr}
The last section built the blocks out of CG tensors. Here we look at the structure behind the tree: the two moves that relabel it, and what they imply. 

\subsection{The two moves on a tree}
Two moves act on a tree. The first re-brackets it, which is the $F$ symbol of Section~\ref{sec:cg}: the left tree below couples $a_1,a_2$ first to the inner charge $b$, the right one couples $a_2,a_3$ first to $b'$, and the entries of $F^{c}_{a_1a_2a_3}$ are the amplitudes of that move,

\subsection{The identities that define the symbols}
The two symbols are not independent. Each is a change of basis, and changes of basis must compose consistently: two routes between the same pair of trees must give the same matrix. There are two minimal such squares of trees, one for each move, and they give the two identities that a pair $(F,R)$ has to satisfy.

    \sum_{v} (F^{d}_{abc})_{uv}\,(R_{ac}^{v})\,(F^{d}_{cab})_{vw} = (R_{ab}^{u})\,(F^{d}_{bac})_{uw}\,(R_{bc}^{w}).

For a representation neither identity is an extra assumption. $F$ is an overlap of CG tensors, and both sides of the pentagon are the same overlap computed in two ways, so the identity follows for free --- for $\mathsf{SU}(2)$ it is the Biedenharn--Elliott relation among the $6j$ symbols, and the hexagon is the corresponding relation between the $6j$ and the $R$ phases. 

As we will see later, the only information we need to compute any tensor is the fusion rules, their duals, and the two symbols. \begin{definition}
    A \emph{fusion category} is a set of sectors/charges $\{1,a,b,\dots\}$ with the fusion rules $a\otimes b=\sum_c N_{ab}^c\,c$ and duals $\bar{a}$, together with a pair of symbols $(F,R)$ satisfying the pentagon and hexagon identities.
\end{definition}
Group representations are a special case of fusion categories, but the definition is far more general. 

There is also a freedom in the symbols. Multiplying every vector of a copy by a phase (by a unitary matrix when the copy space is more than one-dimensional) changes both $F$ and $R$ while leaving every invariant unchanged, so the pair is defined only up to that gauge. For $\mathsf{SU}(2)$ this is the arbitrariness of the CG phase convention, and it is why different tables differ by signs and phases while agreeing on observables.

\subsection{One shape is enough}
Any two trees over the same ordered legs differ by a sequence of re-bracketings and exchanges: an $F$ moves an inner charge from one edge to the next, an $R$ exchanges two neighbouring legs, and a leg glued to the trivial charge --- a bend --- is a single $F$ entry contracted with the trivial charge, which comes out with weight $\sqrt{d_a d_b/d_c}$ and, for a bent leg that is a dual, a Frobenius--Schur phase. Since a sequence of these moves reaches any labelling, it is enough to store one shape. The engine keeps the left-nested chain, $(((l_1l_2)l_3)\cdots)l_r$, so that the only freedom left in a block is which leg sits at which position.

\subsection{Nothing else is needed}
Every coefficient of every block is, after the last two sections, a product of entries of $F$ and $R$; the CG coefficients never appear individually again. A tensor engine can therefore be handed the fusion rules, the duals, $F$ and $R$, and nothing else. That is exactly the interface of the library, and it is why the same code runs for $\mathbb Z_n$, $\mathsf{SU}(2)$, $\mathsf{SU}(3)$ and for a category with no CG coefficients at all, such as the Fibonacci category, whose only objects are $\mathbf{1}$ and $\tau$ with $\tau\otimes\tau=\mathbf{1}\oplus\tau$, quantum dimension $\phi$, and $F$ and $R$ given directly.
