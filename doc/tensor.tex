\chapter{Tensors and symmetries}
\section{How to read this chapter}
\label{sec:roadmap}

This chapter answers one question: how do we store and multiply a tensor that
is constrained by a symmetry, so that we never store or multiply the parts the
symmetry forbids?

We reach the answer in five moves.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{The object and the constraint.}
    Sections~\ref{sec:tensor} and~\ref{sec:symmetry} define a tensor as a
    multilinear map and a symmetry as a group action on its legs.  The smallest
    example, a $\mathrm{U}(1)$ charge, already shows the payoff: a
    symmetry-carrying tensor is block-sparse, and the zero blocks never need to
    exist.
  \item \emph{The representation theory.}
    Sections~\ref{sec:lie}--\ref{sec:we} collect what we need from the Lie
    algebras $\SU{2}$ and $\SU{3}$: how their irreducible representations are
    labelled, how two of them fuse, and why the whole construction collapses
    onto a small set of invariant ``Clebsch--Gordan'' tensors through the
    Wigner--Eckart theorem.
  \item \emph{The data structure and the code.}
    Section~\ref{sec:container} turns that mathematics into a concrete object
    --- a tensor whose entries are dense blocks addressed by symmetry labels ---
    and Section~\ref{sec:code} shows where that object lives in the code, ending
    with one $\SU{2}$ contraction traced from the interface down to a single
    dense multiply.
  \item \emph{What the weights really need.}
    Sections~\ref{sec:category} and~\ref{sec:diagram} strip the group away: a
    fusion category is a pair of tables --- fusion rules and a recoupling --- and
    the contraction algorithm reads only those, so the same code contracts a
    category whose quantum dimension is not an integer.
  \item \emph{The workhorse, and the numbers.}
    Section~\ref{sec:charge-flow} exhibits the charge-conserving contraction and
    singular-value split that every sweep is built from, and
    Section~\ref{sec:anchors} closes with the numbers that verify the claims.
\end{enumerate}

The code is layered to mirror this narrative; Section~\ref{sec:code} gives the
exact map from each concept to a file and a function.

\section{What is a tensor}
\label{sec:tensor}

A tensor is a multilinear map
\begin{equation}
  T \;:\; V_1\times V_2 \times \cdots \times V_k \;\longrightarrow\;
  \mathbb{C},
  \label{eq:tensor-map}
\end{equation}
where each $V_i$ is a vector space.  Physically, these are local Hilbert spaces or its dual. ``Multilinear''
means linear in each argument separately.  Choosing a basis $\{e_{a_i}\}$ in
each space fixes the tensor by its components
\begin{equation}
  T_{a_1 a_2 \cdots a_k}
  \;=\; T\bigl(e_{a_1}, e_{a_2}, \dots, e_{a_k}\bigr),
\end{equation}
and the number of indices, $k$, is the \emph{rank} of the tensor.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[thick]
    \node[draw, minimum size=0.9cm] (T) at (0,0) {$T$};
    \draw (T.north) -- ++(0,0.6) node[above] {$a$};
    \draw (T.west)  -- ++(-0.6,0) node[left]  {$b$};
    \draw (T.east)  -- ++(0.6,0)  node[right] {$c$};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{A rank-$3$ tensor $T_{abc}$ drawn as a node with one leg per index.  A leg can
  point in or out; Section~\ref{sec:container} makes this orientation precise
  as a sign on the leg.}
  \label{fig:tensor-legs}
\end{figure}

Two special cases recur throughout.  A rank-$2$ tensor is a matrix; its
components $M_{ab}$ form a rectangular array.  A rank-$3$ tensor $T_{abc}$ can
be read as a linear map from $V_b \otimes V_c$ into $V_a$, that is, as a stack
of matrices indexed by $a$.

\emph{Example (index contraction).}  Let $B$ be rank-$3$ and $A$ rank-$2$.
Contracting $A$'s first index with $B$'s third gives a new rank-$3$ tensor
\begin{equation}
  C_{abc} \;=\; \sum_{d} B_{abd}\, A_{dc}.
\end{equation}
In Einstein notation the sum is left implicit --- a repeated index is summed ---
so we write $C_{abc}=B_{abd}A_{dc}$.  The code spells the same operation out in
``einsum'' notation: \code{einsum("abd,dc->abc", B, A)}.  Every operation in
this chapter --- contraction, projection, singular-value decomposition --- is a
composition of such contractions, and the symmetry machinery only reorganises
\emph{which} small dense matrices get multiplied, never the arithmetic itself.

A tensor is stored as one dense array of shape $(d_1, d_2, \dots, d_k)$ with
$d_i=\dim V_i$.  That storage is what Section~\ref{sec:container} will replace
with a list of blocks once a symmetry is present.

\section{Symmetry and representations}
\label{sec:symmetry}

A symmetry of a tensor is a group $G$ together with, on each leg, a
representation
\begin{equation}
  \rho_i \;:\; G \;\longrightarrow\; \mathrm{GL}(V_i),
\end{equation}
i.e.\ a homomorphism from $G$ into the invertible linear maps on $V_i$.  The
tensor is \emph{invariant} under $G$ when acting with $G$ on all legs at once
leaves it unchanged:
\begin{equation}
  T \;=\; \rho_1(g) \otimes \rho_2(g) \otimes \cdots \otimes \rho_k(g)\;
  \bigl[\,T\,\bigr]
  \qquad \text{for all } g\in G.
  \label{eq:invariant}
\end{equation}
This single equation is the source of every block structure in the chapter.

A representation is \emph{reducible} if $V_i$ has a nontrivial subspace
invariant under every $\rho_i(g)$; otherwise it is \emph{irreducible} (an
``irrep'').  Maschke's theorem lets us split any finite-dimensional
representation into a direct sum of irreps,
\begin{equation}
  V \;=\; \bigoplus_{a}\, V_a^{\oplus m_a},
\end{equation}
where the index $a$ labels the irrep and $m_a$ counts how many times it appears.
The $a$'s are the \emph{sectors} (or \emph{charges}) of the theory, and from
now on a ``leg'' means a vector space together with a chosen decomposition into
sectors.

Schur's lemma is the engine.  If $\rho_a$ and $\rho_b$ are irreps, a linear map
$M: V_a \to V_b$ that intertwines them ($M\rho_a(g)=\rho_b(g)M$ for all $g$) is
either zero (when $a\neq b$) or a scalar multiple of the identity (when
$a=b$).  Equivalently, the only invariant map between two different sectors is
the zero map.  Invariance~\eqref{eq:invariant} therefore forces the tensor to
vanish on every combination of sectors that does not fuse to the trivial charge,
and to act as a fixed (Clebsch--Gordan) tensor on the combinations that do.
Dense entries that ``cannot happen'' are exactly zero --- not small, zero --- so
they never need to be stored or multiplied.

\emph{Example (a $\mathrm{U}(1)$ charge).}  Let $G=\mathrm{U}(1)$.  Its irreps
are one-dimensional and labelled by an integer charge $q$, with
$\rho_q(e^{i\theta})=e^{iq\theta}$.  A rank-$3$ tensor with legs of charge
$q_1,q_2,q_3$ transforms by the phase $e^{i(q_1+q_2+q_3)\theta}$, so
invariance demands $q_1+q_2+q_3=0$; any component with a different charge
combination is zero.  A rank-$2$ tensor (a matrix) is invariant only when
$q_1=q_2$, so it is block-diagonal: one block per charge, and nothing else.
This is the whole story in miniature --- and for $\SU{2}$ and $\SU{3}$ the
blocks are larger than $1\times 1$, but the principle is identical.

\section{Lie algebras and their representations}
\label{sec:lie}

The symmetries we use are continuous, so their irreps are classified by the
corresponding Lie algebra.

\paragraph{$\SU{2}$.}  The algebra $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ has generators $J_x, J_y,
J_z$ with $[J_i,J_j]=i\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$.  Its irreps are labelled by spin $j$
with dimension $2j+1$ and quadratic Casimir $j(j+1)$.  Throughout this document
--- and in the code --- we record the sector by the integer $2j$, so that
sector $s$ carries spin $j=s/2$ and has dimension
\begin{equation}
  \dim V_s \;=\; s+1.
\end{equation}
Sector $0$ is the singlet, $1$ the doublet, $2$ the triplet, $4$ the quintet,
and so on.  The Casimir of sector $s$ is $\tfrac{s}{2}\bigl(\tfrac{s}{2}+1\bigr)$.

\paragraph{$\SU{3}$.}  The algebra $\mathfrak{su}(3)$ has rank $2$, so its
irreps are labelled by a pair of non-negative integers, the Dynkin labels
$(p,q)$.  The fundamental is $(1,0)$ (dimension $3$), its conjugate $(0,1)$
(dimension $\bar{3}$), and the adjoint is $(1,1)$ (dimension $8$).  The
dimension and quadratic Casimir of $(p,q)$ are
\begin{equation}
  \dim V_{(p,q)} \;=\; \frac{(p+1)(q+1)(p+q+2)}{2},
  \qquad
  C_2(p,q) \;=\; \frac{p^2+q^2+pq+3p+3q}{3}.
  \label{eq:su3}
\end{equation}

\emph{Example (labels and dimensions).}  The smallest $\SU{3}$ sectors and the
sectors that appear when fusing two adjoints are

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
  \hline
  sector $(p,q)$ & $\dim V$ & $C_2$ \\ \hline
  $(0,0)$ & $1$  & $0$ \\
  $(1,0)$ & $3$  & $4/3$ \\
  $(0,1)$ & $3$  & $4/3$ \\
  $(1,1)$ & $8$  & $3$ \\
  $(2,0)$ & $6$  & $10/3$ \\
  $(3,0)$ & $10$ & $6$ \\
  $(0,3)$ & $10$ & $6$ \\
  $(2,2)$ & $27$ & $8$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Both formulae in~\eqref{eq:su3} are checked against every sector with
$p+q\le 4$ in Section~\ref{sec:anchors}.

\section{Tensor products, fusion, and Clebsch--Gordan coefficients}
\label{sec:fusion}

The tensor product of two irreps is generally reducible:
\begin{equation}
  V_a \otimes V_b \;=\; \bigoplus_{c}\; V_c^{\oplus N^{c}_{ab}},
  \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
where the non-negative integers $N^{c}_{ab}$ are the fusion multiplicities and
the sum runs over sectors $c$ that ``can appear'' in the product.  Equation
\eqref{eq:fusion} is the \emph{fusion rule} of the pair $(a,b)$; a choice of
basis realising it is a set of \emph{Clebsch--Gordan} (CG) tensors.

For each triple $(a,b,c)$ with $N^{c}_{ab}>0$ we fix an embedding
\begin{equation}
  C^{abc}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
  \;:\; V_c \;\hookrightarrow\; V_a \otimes V_b,
  \qquad
  \sum_{\alpha\beta} C^{abc}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,
  C^{abc}_{\alpha\beta\gamma'} \;=\; \delta_{\gamma\gamma'},
  \label{eq:cg}
\end{equation}
so the CG tensor is an isometry with the normalisation
$\lVert C^{abc}\rVert^2 = \dim V_c$ (its $\alpha,\beta$ indices span $\dim V_a
\cdot \dim V_b$ entries, but only $\dim V_c$ of them survive as the embedded
image of $V_c$).  This normalisation, $\lVert C\rVert^2=\dim V_c$, is the one
the code enforces and the one Section~\ref{sec:anchors} verifies numerically.

\emph{Example ($\SU{2}$ fusion).}  In terms of the sector label $s=2j$,
\begin{equation}
  \tfrac12\otimes\tfrac12 \;=\; 0\oplus 1
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  1\otimes 1 \;=\; 0\oplus 2,
  \qquad
  1\otimes 1 \;=\; 0\oplus 1\oplus 2
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  2\otimes 2 \;=\; 0\oplus 2\oplus 4 .
\end{equation}
The CG tensor $C^{224}$, embedding the quintet $V_4$ into the two triplets
$V_2\otimes V_2$, has squared norm $5=\dim V_4$.

\emph{Example ($\SU{3}$ fusion of two adjoints).}  With $(1,1)$ the adjoint,
\begin{equation}
  8\otimes 8 \;=\; 27 \oplus 10 \oplus \overline{10} \oplus 2\cdot 8 \oplus 1,
  \quad\text{i.e.}\quad
  (1,1)\otimes(1,1) \;=\; (2,2)\oplus(3,0)\oplus(0,3)\oplus 2(1,1)\oplus(0,0).
\end{equation}
The multiplicity $N^{(1,1)}_{(1,1),(1,1)}=2$ is the first place the story
diverges from $\SU{2}$: two copies of the adjoint appear, and they must be
distinguished.

When $N^{c}_{ab}>1$ a single charge label no longer identifies a block, so the
code attaches a copy index.  The set of embeddings for a whole leg of sectors
is a \emph{fusion tree}: a tree whose leaves are the leg sectors and whose
internal vertices carry the intermediate charges.  Figure~\ref{fig:fusion-tree}
shows the tree for three sectors $a,b,c$ fusing to a total charge $e$.

  \label{fig:fusion-tree}

Beyond the CG coefficients, three related objects appear and are provided by
the group layer (Section~\ref{sec:code}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{$6j$ symbols}, which re-couple three sectors: the two trees
    fusing $(a,b)\to x$ then $(x,c)\to e$, versus $(b,c)\to y$ then
    $(a,y)\to e$, are related by a unitary matrix in $x,y$ --- the $6j$ symbol.
    The code uses them (and their generalisations) when a tree has to be
    reshaped to contract two tensors.
  \item \emph{Conjugate representations.}  The dual $V_a^*$ is generally a
    different sector $\bar{a}$.  For $\SU{2}$, every irrep is self-conjugate
    ($\bar{s}=s$); for $\SU{3}$, $(p,q)$ and $(q,p)$ are conjugate.  A leg
    records whether it is dual with a sign, the \code{qconj} flag.
  \item \emph{The Frobenius--Schur indicator.}  A self-conjugate sector has an
    invariant bilinear form that is symmetric or antisymmetric; the sign is the
    Frobenius--Schur indicator and affects the sign with which a dual leg is
    folded into a contraction.
\end{itemize}

\section{Invariant tensors and the Wigner--Eckart theorem}
\label{sec:we}

An operator that respects the symmetry is itself an invariant tensor.  The
Wigner--Eckart theorem says that its matrix elements factor: an operator in
sector $k$ between states of sectors $a,b$ is
\begin{equation}
  \langle a,\alpha \mid T^k_q \mid b,\beta \rangle
  \;=\;
  C^{a k b}_{\alpha q \beta}\;
  \langle a \lVert T^k \rVert b \rangle ,
  \label{eq:we}
\end{equation}
where the CG tensor $C^{akb}$ carries all the geometry and the reduced matrix
element $\langle a \lVert T^k \rVert b \rangle$ carries all the physics.  A
tensor network built only from invariant tensors can therefore be evaluated
``once per sector'': the geometry is a fixed, reusable set of CG tensors, and
the physics is a much smaller collection of reduced matrix elements.

\emph{Example (spin operators).}  On a spin-$j$ site the operators
$\{S_z, S_x, S_y\}$ form a triplet, i.e.\ they transform in sector $k=2$.  For
a spin-$\tfrac12$ site (sector $a=b=1$) the CG tensor $C^{121}$ is the Pauli
matrix up to scale, and the reduced matrix element is the single number
$\langle \tfrac12 \lVert S \rVert \tfrac12 \rangle = \sqrt{3/2}$.  Reassembling
via \eqref{eq:we} reproduces $\vec S = \tfrac12 \vec\sigma$.  This is exactly
what the code does: it stores, per sector, the reduced operators
(\code{OperatorContent.tensor_operators}), and rebuilds the full invariant
operator on demand.

Because the CG tensor is reused, any two-site object built from the same sector
can be precomputed once.  For a sector $s$ of dimension $\dim s$, define the
\emph{channel} --- the unique (up to scale) invariant operator on
$V_s\otimes V_s$ in that sector --- by
\begin{equation}
  C_s \;=\; \sum_{m,m'} D^{s}_{mm'}\; T_m \otimes T_{m'},
  \label{eq:channel}
\end{equation}
where $D^{s}$ is the invariant bilinear form (the metric of the sector).  The
code exposes this through \code{OperatorContent}: \code{channel} builds the
object, and \code{normalize} rescales it so that $\lVert C_s\rVert^2 = \dim s$,
matching the CG normalisation \eqref{eq:cg}.

\section{The block-sparse container}
\label{sec:container}

We now replace the dense array of Section~\ref{sec:tensor} with an explicit
block structure.  Three objects do the work: \code{Leg}, \code{FusionTree}, and
\code{Tensor}.

\paragraph{Leg.}  A \code{Leg} is one index together with its symmetry data:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{sectors}: the ordered list of sector labels present on this leg;
  \item \code{slices}: the boundaries of each sector's entries in the dense
    layout --- for a leg of total dimension $d$, the entries of sector $i$
    occupy rows $[\mathrm{slices}[i], \mathrm{slices}[i+1])$;
  \item \code{qconj}: the orientation, $+1$ or $-1$, telling whether the leg
    is used as $V$ or $V^*$.
\end{itemize}
A sector $i$ on the leg carries a block of physical dimension
$\mathrm{block\_dim}(i)$, which is $m_i \cdot \dim(s_i)$: $m_i$ degenerate
copies of the sector $s_i$.  The \emph{multiplicity} is
$m_i = \mathrm{block\_dim}(i)/\dim(s_i)$, and the leg's total number of
multiplets, $\sum_i m_i$, is the invariant dimension the code calls the
``QSpace dimension''.

\emph{Example (one concrete leg).}  A bond leg on $\SU{2}$ whose sectors are
$s=(0,2,4)$ --- singlet, triplet, quintet --- with multiplicities $m=(1,2,1)$
and orientation $+1$ is, in the reduced mode, the object
\begin{center}
\code{Leg}(\code{group}, \code{sectors}=[0,2,4], \code{slices}=[0,1,7,12],
\code{qconj}=+1),
\end{center}
with \code{dim}$=12$ and \code{multiplets}$=4$.  The fields resolve sector by
sector as
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
  \hline
  sector $s$ & $\dim V_s$ & multiplicity $m$ & block dimension & dense rows \\
  \hline
  $0$ & $1$ & $1$ & $1$ & $[0,1)$ \\
  $2$ & $3$ & $2$ & $6$ & $[1,7)$ \\
  $4$ & $5$ & $1$ & $5$ & $[7,12)$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The \emph{block dimension} of a sector is $m\cdot\dim V_s$, and \code{slices}
holds its running total, so in the dense layout the singlet is row~$0$, the two
triplets occupy rows $1$--$6$, and the quintet rows $7$--$11$.  The stored
content, however, is only the multiplicity: \code{multiplicity} returns
$(1,2,1)$, while the block dimension is what the dense layout would use.  That
difference --- multiplicity versus block dimension --- is where the compression
lives.

\paragraph{FusionTree.}  A \code{FusionTree} records how a tuple of sectors
fuses to a total charge: the intermediate charges and, where multiplicities
occur, the copy index at each vertex (Figure~\ref{fig:fusion-tree}).  It is the
address of a single ``multiplet'' block.  A \code{FusionTree} can be expanded
to a dense block --- the contraction of the CG tensors along its vertices ---
and this expansion is cached.

\emph{Example (one working tree).}  Fusing three triplets to a singlet on
$\SU{2}$ is the single tree
\begin{center}
\code{FusionTree}(\code{group}, \code{sectors}=[2,2,2], \code{inner}=[2],
\code{coupled}=0),
\end{center}
whose \code{chain()}$=[2,2,0]$: the first two triplets fuse to the intermediate
triplet $2$, which then fuses with the third to reach total charge $0$.
Expanding the tree with \code{to_dense_block} contracts two CG tensors along
that chain,
\begin{equation}
  B_{m_1 m_2 m_3}
  \;=\; \sum_{\mu} C^{(2,2,2)}_{m_1 m_2 \mu}\; C^{(2,2,0)}_{\mu m_3},
\end{equation}
where $C^{(2,2,2)}$ couples two triplets to a triplet and $C^{(2,2,0)}$ couples
a triplet to the singlet.  In the standard Condon--Shortley phases the nonzero
entries, with $m\in\{+1,0,-1\}$, are
\begin{equation}
  C^{(2,2,2)}_{1,0,+1} = C^{(2,2,2)}_{1,-1,0} = C^{(2,2,2)}_{0,-1,-1}
    = +1/\sqrt{2},
  \qquad
  C^{(2,2,2)}_{0,1,+1} = C^{(2,2,2)}_{-1,1,0} = C^{(2,2,2)}_{-1,0,-1}
    = -1/\sqrt{2},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  C^{(2,2,0)}_{1,-1} = C^{(2,2,0)}_{-1,1} = +1/\sqrt{3},
  \qquad
  C^{(2,2,0)}_{0,0} = -1/\sqrt{3},
\end{equation}
so $\lVert C^{(2,2,2)}\rVert^2 = 3 = \dim V_2$ and
$\lVert C^{(2,2,0)}\rVert^2 = 1 = \dim V_0$, exactly \eqref{eq:cg}.  The
contraction is then the fully antisymmetric Levi-Civita tensor,
\begin{equation}
  B_{m_1 m_2 m_3} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\,\varepsilon_{m_1 m_2 m_3},
  \qquad \lVert B\rVert^2 = 1 = \dim V_0,
\end{equation}
the unique invariant in $V_2^{\otimes3}$ --- for instance
$B_{1,0,-1}=+1/\sqrt6$ and $B_{0,1,-1}=-1/\sqrt6$.  That $3\times3\times3$
array is the dense expansion of this tree; in the reduced mode the tree stores
only its address --- one number --- and the two CG tensors are shared with every
other triplet in the system.

\paragraph{Tensor.}  A \code{Tensor} is a collection of dense blocks plus the
bookkeeping that says where each block lives:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{legs}: one \code{Leg} per index;
  \item \code{blocks}: the dense sub-tensors, one per allowed sector
    combination;
  \item \code{qdata}: for each block, a key $(\text{legs}, \text{inner},
    \text{copies})$ --- the block indices on each leg, the intermediate charges,
    and the copy indices of the underlying fusion tree;
  \item \code{qtotal}: the total charge the legs fuse to (trivial for a
    physical, symmetry-invariant tensor).
\end{itemize}
A block is allowed only if its sectors fuse to \code{qtotal}; every other
combination is absent.  This is Schur's lemma made into an address scheme.

Every tensor is stored in one mode, the \emph{reduced} one: each block holds
only the multiplicity indices, one number per multiplet, and the CG tensors are
kept separately in the fusion tree.  The physical components of a block are
recovered by expanding its tree, which is what \code{stored_dense} caches; a
contraction never materialises them, it weighs each output tree by the
recoupling factor of Section~\ref{sec:charge-flow} and multiplies the reduced
blocks directly.

\emph{Example (one $\SU{2}$ tensor).}  Take a rank-$2$ tensor whose two legs
both carry sectors $\{0,2\}$ (singlet and triplet) with multiplicity one, and
total charge $0$.  Fusion allows only the pairs $0\otimes0\to0$ and
$2\otimes2\to0$, so the tensor is two blocks: a $1\times1$ block and a
$3\times3$ block.  The dense array would be $4\times4=16$ numbers; the
block-sparse tensor stores $1+9=10$, and the CG tensor for the triplet block is
shared with every other triplet in the system.  For large bond dimensions the
saving is not 40\% but orders of magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:anchors}).

\section{How the code is organised}
\label{sec:code}

The implementation is a stack of four layers, each importing only the layer
below it, mirroring Sections~\ref{sec:lie}--\ref{sec:container}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Fusion category} (\path{mpsdmrg/symmetry/cat/cat.h}).  A
    \code{FusionCategory} knows its labels and how they combine, and it carries
    the data a symmetric network recouples with:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \code{fusion_outcomes(a, b)} --- the list of $c$ in
        \eqref{eq:fusion};
      \item \code{n_symbol(a, b, c)} --- the multiplicity $N^{c}_{ab}$;
      \item \code{can_fuse_to(a, b, c)} --- whether $N^{c}_{ab}>0$;
      \item \code{associator(a, b, c, d, e, f)} --- the amplitudes $F$ of
        Section~\ref{sec:charge-flow} that rewrite the coupling $(ab)c\to d$ through
        $e$ into $a(bc)\to d$ through $f$, one entry per combination of the four
        multiplicities, so that a graph contraction reads each copy on its own;
      \item \code{braiding(a, b, c)} --- what exchanging the two legs of one
        vertex costs;
      \item \code{sector_dim(a)} --- the dimension of a label, the number of
        states in one copy of its multiplet;
      \item \code{quantum_dim(a)} --- the same number as a real, the weight a
        closed loop carries; it equals \code{sector_dim} for a group and may be
        non-integer for a general category;
      \item \code{is_trivial} --- whether the category is the trivial one;
      \item \code{braiding_style} --- \code{bosonic}, \code{fermionic} or
        \code{anyonic}, the exchange behaviour the associator and the braiding
        realize (Fibonacci is anyonic);
      \item \code{clear_caches()} --- drop the memo tables a category keeps;
      \item \code{fusion_tensor(a, b, c, device)} --- the dense realization of
        one vertex, the CG tensor \eqref{eq:cg} for a group, normalised to
        $\lVert C\rVert^2=\dim V_c$;
      \item \code{dual_sector(a)}, \code{dual_sign(a)}, \code{dual_phase(a)}
        --- the conjugate label and its Frobenius--Schur data.
    \end{itemize}
    The tensor engine includes this header only, so what a concrete symmetry is
    stays behind the interface.  A group is the subclass
    (\path{mpsdmrg/symmetry/cat/group.h}) whose associator and braiding follow
    from its CG calculus: \code{Group::associator} is the overlap of the two
    fusion trees in the CG basis, the $6j$ symbol, and \code{Group::braiding}
    is the exchange sign.  The interface also declares the CG-basis metric
    \code{invariant_form(sector, device)}, the metric $D^{s}$ of
    \eqref{eq:channel}, which a category with no CG basis does not implement and
    raises for.  The concrete groups are subclasses of \code{Group}:
    \code{u1_symmetry} and \code{z2_symmetry} (abelian), \code{su2_symmetry}
    and \code{su3_symmetry} (non-abelian).
    Abelian groups have $\dim V_s=1$ for all $s$, so their fusion trees are
    trivial and the machinery reduces to integer charge bookkeeping.
  \item \textbf{Leg and FusionTree} (\path{mpsdmrg/symmetry/tree/tree.cpp}).
    The \code{Leg} and \code{FusionTree} of Section~\ref{sec:container}, plus
    the constructors \code{site_leg} (a leg of $d$ copies of the trivial sector, an abelian
    group only), \code{multiplet_leg} (a leg of one multiplet, named by its sector),
    \code{bond_leg} (a leg built from explicit sectors), and \code{fuse_legs}.
  \item \textbf{Tensor} (\path{mpsdmrg/tensor/tensor.cpp}).  The
    \code{Tensor} container, its \code{from_torch} constructor, and the
    contraction engine \code{cached_einsum}.  A contraction is planned once (the \code{opt_einsum}
    contraction path is cached) and then executed block by block: for each
    allowed combination of input blocks the engine (a) reshapes by the fusion
    trees, (b) multiplies the small dense blocks with \code{at::einsum}, and
    (c) re-weights each output tree by its recoupling factor, read off the
    associator and the braiding where the layout is covered and from the CG
    expansion otherwise.  Tree expansions and weights are themselves cached,
    so the per-block cost is one dense multiply.
  \item \textbf{Linalg} (\path{mpsdmrg/linalg/}).  The symmetry-aware linear
    algebra: \code{svd}, \code{qr}, \code{rq}, \code{norm}, \code{inner},
    \code{trace}, and \code{absorb}.  These call the group layer to split a
    tensor by charge, run a standard dense decomposition per charge, and
    reassemble the result as a new \code{Tensor} (Section~\ref{sec:charge-flow}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Example: contracting two $\SU{2}$ tensors.}
\label{sec:contract-example}

To watch the whole engine at once, contract two tensors over a single leg,
\begin{equation}
  C_{pqc} \;=\; \sum_{x} A_{pqx}\, B_{xc},
  \qquad \text{\code{einsum("pqx,xc->pqc", A, B)}},
\end{equation}
with $G=\SU{2}$ and every tensor carrying total charge $0$.  Concretely: $A$
has legs $(p,q,x)$ with $p=q=2$ (two triplets) and a bond $x\in\{0,2\}$
(singlet and triplet); $B$ has legs $(x,c)$ with $x,c\in\{0,2\}$.  The output
$C$ therefore has legs $(p,q,c)=(2,2,c)$.

\textbf{The operands are already trees.}  Each block of $A$ is keyed by a
\code{FusionTree} that \code{fusion_trees} built from the leg sectors and the
total charge.  Fusing $2\otimes2$ yields $\{0,2,4\}$, and then fusing with the
bond sector must reach $0$, which forces the inner charge to equal the bond
sector: $A$ has one block per bond sector, with inner charge $x$, and its
reduced entry is a single number $a_x$.  The dense block is $a_x$ times the
shared Clebsch--Gordan expansion of that tree.  $B$ is rank-$2$, so its trees
are trivial; fusing $x$ with $c$ to $0$ leaves $B$ nonzero only on the diagonal
$x=c$, with reduced entries $b_0,b_2$.

\textbf{The contraction matches shared sectors, then builds the output tree.}
The engine walks the summed label $x\in\{0,2\}$; the diagonal form of $B$ keeps
only $x=c$, so each output block receives exactly one contribution.  At the same
time it enumerates the \emph{output} tree from scratch with \code{fusion_trees}
over the output sectors $(2,2,c)$:
\begin{equation}
  c=0:\ 2\otimes2\to\{0,2,4\},\ y\otimes0\ni0 \Rightarrow y=0,
  \qquad
  c=2:\ 2\otimes2\to\{0,2,4\},\ y\otimes2\ni0 \Rightarrow y=2 .
\end{equation}
Two trees survive, with inner charges $0$ and $2$.  The tree is constructed
from the output legs, not copied from the operands.

\textbf{The weight is the overlap of the trees.}  The engine folds the dense
expansions of $A$ and $B$ over the shared leg (\code{fold_operands}) and weights
each output tree by its recoupling factor, one scalar per tree:
\begin{equation}
  C^{(2,2,c)} \;=\; a_c\, b_c\, w_c \;\cdot\;
  \bigl[\text{CG expansion of the output tree}\bigr].
\end{equation}
For $c=0$ the shared leg is a singlet and $w_0=1$ exactly (the singlet CG tensor
has unit norm).  For $c=2$ the overlap re-couples the three triplet pairings,
so $w_2$ is the $6j$ symbol of $\SU{2}$ --- the coefficient that re-expresses
the operand trees in the output tree's coupling order.  This is the general
case: every output tree is weighted by such a recoupling factor, computed once
per tree, so the per-block cost of the contraction stays a single dense multiply
(\code{at::einsum}).

The factor itself is read from the category: the two operands are permuted into
the output's leg order with one associator and one braiding per swap of adjacent
legs, their shared couplings are matched, and the junction where the two free
parts meet contributes one associator and the loop weight of the shared block
(Section~\ref{sec:diagram}).  The overlap of the dense expansions
(\code{stored_dense} and \code{tree_weight}) remains as the fallback for a shape
the categorical rule does not cover --- a leg both operands keep, or a fusion
multiplicity that pairs the associators into matrices over copies.  The
tree-and-weight bookkeeping is the algorithm of \cite{Weichselbaum_2012}.

Above these four sits the model layer (\path{mpsdmrg/mps.py},
\path{mpsdmrg/mpo.py}, \path{mpsdmrg/broomstick.py}), which builds the
site/bond legs of a physical system and drives the DMRG sweeps; it never talks
to matrices directly, only through the \code{Tensor} API.

\emph{The route, end to end.}  A tensor enters through \code{Tensor.from_torch}
and is stored reduced (multiplicities plus trees).  Every algebraic operation
(\code{cached_einsum}, \code{svd}, \code{qr}) is written to work on the reduced
form, and the layer boundary is that form: the physical components of a block
are never materialised, only the tree expansion of a single output tree is, and
only where the categorical rule declines a layout
(Section~\ref{sec:diagram}) does the dense expansion come back.

\section{From the group picture to a category}
\label{sec:category}

Everything up to here was built on a group: a block is addressed by a
\code{FusionTree}, whose dense value is a Clebsch--Gordan expansion, and every
weight is an overlap in that basis.  A group is not the smallest input the
weights need, and this section separates the two: it walks one exact fusion
through the engine and marks which ingredient the weight consumes, and which is
only a convenient way of writing that ingredient down.

\paragraph{One exact fusion, with numbers.}
Take three spin-one legs, $a=b=c=1$, in the sector labels of
Section~\ref{sec:fusion} (the label is $s=2J$, so a spin-one leg is $2$ and
\code{sector_dim(s) = s + 1}).  Coupling the first two legs gives
$1\otimes1=0\oplus1\oplus2$, and the total fixes the third coupling; the two
orders in which the three legs can be coupled are related by
\begin{equation}
  \bigl|(ab)c\to d\bigr\rangle
  \;=\;\sum_{f}
  F^{abc}_{d}(e,f)\,\bigl|a(bc)\to d\bigr\rangle ,
  \label{eq:F}
\end{equation}
and the exchange of the two legs of one vertex by the braiding $R^{ab}_{c}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:tree}).  In the engine these are
\code{FusionCategory::associator} and \code{FusionCategory::braiding}.  For
$d=2$ and the three legs $2,2,2$ the associator is the symmetric matrix
\begin{equation}
  F^{2 2 2}_{2}(e,f)=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \tfrac13 & -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3} & \tfrac{\sqrt5}{3}\\[2pt]
    -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3} & \tfrac12 & \tfrac{\sqrt5}{2\sqrt3}\\[2pt]
    \tfrac{\sqrt5}{3} & \tfrac{\sqrt5}{2\sqrt3} & \tfrac16
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad e,f\in\{0,2,4\},
  \label{eq:F-example}
\end{equation}
and the braiding for the same pair of legs is
$R^{2 2}_{0}=+1$, $R^{2 2}_{2}=-1$, $R^{2 2}_{4}=+1$: the symmetric coupling is
even under exchange, the antisymmetric one odd, and the symmetric $f=4$ even
again.  Both tables are what the categorical route reads, and the checks of
Section~\ref{sec:anchors} pin them to machine precision.

\paragraph{What the weight actually consumes.}
Nothing in Equations~\eqref{eq:F} and \eqref{eq:F-example} refers to a
Clebsch--Gordan coefficient.  The engine's \code{Group} happens to compute the
two tables \emph{from} them --- \code{Group::associator} is the dense overlap of
the two coupling orders and \code{Group::braiding} the overlap of a vertex with
its own leg exchange (\path{mpsdmrg/symmetry/cat/group.cpp}) --- but the
interface never asks for a vertex, only for these numbers.  Measuring the call
graph makes the split explicit: what a symmetric contraction of the engine needs
is exactly
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \code{fusion_outcomes(a, b)} and \code{n_symbol(a, b, c)} --- the fusion
    rules with their multiplicities, i.e.\ which couplings are allowed at all;
  \item \code{quantum_dim(c)} --- the weight of a closed loop, and
    \code{sector_dim(c)} --- the extent of one block, equal to it for a group;
  \item \code{associator} --- the $F$ of \eqref{eq:F}, one entry per combination
    of the four multiplicities;
  \item \code{braiding} --- the $R$ of Figure~\ref{fig:tree};
  \item \code{dual_sector}, \code{dual_sign} --- the cup and the cap, i.e.\ which
    label is the conjugate of which and what closing a pair of dual legs costs.
\end{enumerate}
and what it does \emph{not} need is the Clebsch--Gordan tensor, the invariant
form, or integer dimensions.  Those belong to the group that supplies the
associator and the braiding, not to the contraction that consumes them.

  \caption{The recoupling $\eqref{eq:F}$ as a tensor diagram.  Left: the coupling
  order $(ab)c\to d$ through $e$.  Right: the same total charge through $f$.
  The amplitude of the move is the associator $F^{abc}_{d}(e,f)$; exchanging the
  two legs of either vertex multiplies by the braiding.  A general category is

  \label{fig:tree}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The same machine without a group.}
The Fibonacci category is the sharp test of that claim.  It has two labels,
$1$ and $\tau$, the fusion rule
\begin{equation}
  \tau\otimes\tau \;=\; 1\oplus\tau,\qquad
  \tau\otimes 1=\tau ,
  \label{eq:fib}
\end{equation}
and the quantum dimension $d_{\tau}=\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$, which is not an
integer and not the dimension of any block; $d_1=1$.  There is no
Clebsch--Gordan tensor to overlap, so the category defines the associator and the
braiding directly (\path{mpsdmrg/symmetry/cat/fibonacci}) and is, in the
vocabulary of \code{cat.h}, of \code{FusionStyle::general}, so
\code{carries_multiplicity()} is true even though every multiplicity is $1$.
Nothing in the list above is missing, so the same diagram algorithm of
Section~\ref{sec:diagram} applies --- the loop weight is $\varphi$ where a group
would have had an integer, and the fusion of two $\tau$'s resolves into two
channels (Figure~\ref{fig:exact}).  Representing a tensor as a fusion tree and
contracting two trees by moving one into the other's basis is the organisation
\code{TensorKit} uses \cite{tensorkit}; the algorithm below fixes what such a
move pays at every step, and keeps its dense fallback wherever a multiplicity
would pair the coefficients into a matrix.

  knows how to expand every vertex into Clebsch--Gordan coefficients; the
  categorical route only ever asks for the outcomes and their weights.
  Fibonacci has no such expansion --- $\tau\otimes\tau$ has two channels and its
  quantum dimension is irrational --- yet supplies the same two tables, so the

\section{The diagram algorithm}
\label{sec:diagram}

This is the algorithm \code{diagram\_weight}
(\path{mpsdmrg/tensor/diagram.cpp}) runs, and the one the engine calls per
output block from \code{mpsdmrg/tensor/tensor.cpp}.

\paragraph{The two objects.}
A chain of $n$ legs has couplings $c_1,\dots,c_{n-2}$ --- a nested chain
$l_0l_1l_2\dots$, so $c_k$ is the charge of the first $k+1$ legs --- and one
total charge, the coupling after the last pair.  That is all a chain is, and it
is also the layout the engine hands over: the operand's legs, the coupled charge
of every inner vertex, and its total.  The weight of one output block is the
contraction of the two operand chains glued along their shared legs with the
output chain,
\begin{equation}
  W \;=\; \bigl\langle Y_{\mathrm{out}}\,\bigr|\,
  Y_L\bowtie Y_R\bigr\rangle ,
  \label{eq:diagram}
\end{equation}
with the conventions of Section~\ref{sec:contract-example}: every tree is
normalised to the quantum dimension of its own coupling, a leg with
\code{qconj = -1} carries the duality matrix, the operands' magnetic axes are
summed with weight one, and the output's magnetic axis is pinned at
\code{reference_index}.

\paragraph{Move one: one adjacent swap.}
Only one primitive is needed.  Swapping the legs $x_1,x_2$ of a chain at
position $p$ is one move of the coupling $c_p$; its amplitude is a braiding and
two associators (Figure~\ref{fig:swap}),
\begin{equation}
  M(c_p,c'_p) \;=\; \sum_{f}
  F^{x_0 x_1 x_2}_{c_2}\bigl(c_p,f\bigr)\,R^{x_1x_2}_{f}\,
  F^{x_0 x_2 x_1}_{c_2}\bigl(c'_p,f\bigr) ,
  \label{eq:swap}
\end{equation}
summed over the intermediate charge $f$ of the exchanged pair; for $p=0$ there
is no neighbouring charge and the swap is the pure braiding $R$.  The function
\code{move\_matrix} returns \eqref{eq:swap} as a small matrix over the old and
the new coupling, and every entry is a single associator or braiding value.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{fig-swap}
  \caption{One adjacent swap, \eqref{eq:swap}: the two legs exchange by a
  braiding, bracketed on both sides by the associators that pull the third leg
  back into place.  The whole diagram algorithm is this move, applied until the
  legs are in the order the output wants.}
  \label{fig:swap}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Move two: a permutation is a product of swaps.}
\code{permute\_chain} sorts the legs into the requested order with insertion
sort, and carries a \emph{set} of states rather than one: a chain together with
a coupling tuple is moved by applying \eqref{eq:swap} at each swap and summing
over the couplings the swap can land on.  The result is
\begin{equation}
  \bigl\{\,c \;\longmapsto\; \text{amplitude}\,\bigr\},
  \label{eq:states}
\end{equation}
one entry per coupling tuple the chain can carry in the new leg order, with the
amplitude a product of the moves that produced it.  Both the zero-, one- and
two-shared-leg cases and the general layout go through this same routine; it is
also the reason a chain is stored as a tuple of couplings rather than as a dense
tensor.

\paragraph{The glue.}
The two operands are permuted so that their shared legs come first and their free
legs follow in the output's order: the first legs of the moved chains are the
glued ones, the tail is the free part.  Gluing is then a matching condition ---
the shared block's couplings must agree on both sides --- and a single closed
loop, the shared block itself, whose label $b$ is the total charge of the glued
part (Figure~\ref{fig:diagram}).  Closing that loop is what the engine pays for
with the weight of the block, so the junction scale carries
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{b}}}\;\prod_{i=1}^{n-2}
  F^{b\,\mu_{i-1} f_{i}}_{c_{s+i}}\bigl(c_{s+i-1},\phi_{i}\bigr),
  \qquad \mu_{0}=f_{0},\quad \mu_{i}=\phi_{i},
  \label{eq:junction}
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the number of glued legs, $b=c_{s}$ is their total charge (the
shared block), $c_{s+i}$ is the coupling the left chain carries after its $i$-th
free leg, and $\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n-2}$ are the frame couplings --- the
couplings the free part takes once the block has been moved through it.  One
associator per interior vertex of the left free chain moves the block through it,
each step summed over its intermediate coupling; the right free part needs none,
because the chain move of \eqref{eq:states} already carries it.  A pair whose dual leg lies on the
left also carries the self-duality sign of that label, which is how a
half-integer glued leg keeps the right weight.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{fig-diagram}
  \caption{Equation~\eqref{eq:diagram} as a network: two chains glued along some
  of their legs, and the output chain that caps the free ends.  The glued legs
  are put first by \code{permute\_chain}, so the shared part is a block at the
  head of both chains.}
  \label{fig:diagram}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The joined chain.}
The free parts of the two operands form one chain, in the order (left free legs,
right free legs).  A coupling tuple only names a block in one and the same leg
order, so that joined chain is moved once more into the \emph{output's} leg order
with \eqref{eq:states}, and the weight of one output block is the coefficient of
that block's own coupling tuple:
\begin{equation}
  W \;=\; \sum_{\text{state pairs}}\ \sum_{\phi}\
  \sigma\;\cdot\;\text{(left amplitude)}\cdot\text{(right amplitude)}
  \;\cdot\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{b}}}\,F\bigl(\phi\bigr)\;\cdot\;
  \bigl[\text{joined amplitude for that coupling tuple}\bigr],
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma$ the duality sign of \eqref{eq:junction} and the product in
\eqref{eq:junction} empty when the left free part has fewer than three legs.  Every factor on the right is a fusion rule, an associator, a
braiding or a quantum dimension; no Clebsch--Gordan coefficient and no dense
contraction appears.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{fig-junction}
  \caption{The junction.  The glued part closes into a loop of the shared block,
  which carries the weight $1/\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{blk}}}$; the left free part is
  recoupled into the frame by one associator, and a glued pair whose duality sits
  on the left contributes the self-duality sign of that label.  Everything else
  in \eqref{eq:weight} is the chain move of Figure~\ref{fig:swap}.}
  \label{fig:junction}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{When it declines, and what is measured exact.}
On a two-operand contraction \code{diagram\_weight} computes every layout the
engine forms: the joined chain is built for any number of glued legs, an outer
product with no glued leg is the two chains concatenated through the left
operand's total charge, and a scalar output keeps a term exactly when the two
operands' totals can fuse to the output's total.  It still declines --- and the
caller keeps the dense fallback --- for a leg that both operands keep in the
output (which would be counted twice) and for a fusion multiplicity that pairs
the associators into matrices over copies rather than a scalar.  Those guards
are structural: they name what the surgery assumes, not how far it has been
measured.  Measured in a build with the dense route compiled out
(\code{DENSEPATH=0}), so the numbers are the route's own and no fallback can
produce them: the $22$ layouts of one $L=6$ SU(2) Heisenberg sweep
($1.1\times10^{-15}$), the four shapes that used to decline
(\code{abcde,af->bcdef} $4.4\times10^{-16}$, \code{abcde,ead->bc}
$3.3\times10^{-16}$, \code{abd,ce->abcde} $5.6\times10^{-16}$,
\code{ab,ac->bc} $3.3\times10^{-16}$), the scalar norm of a tensor in the total
sector $(2,)$, and twenty randomised layouts with up to five free and three
glued legs on both sides.

\section{Charge flow: contraction and splitting}
\label{sec:charge-flow}

The object that makes the whole scheme run is a tensor network whose bonds
carry a running charge.  An MPS of length $L$ is a chain of site tensors with a
bond leg between neighbours; because the global state is an invariant tensor,
each bond carries a well-defined total charge that accumulates from left to
right.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[thick]
    \foreach \x/\l in {0/{$T_1$},1/{$T_2$},2/{$T_3$},3/{$T_4$}} {
      \node[draw, minimum size=0.85cm] (T\x) at (\x*1.6,0) {\l};
      \draw (T\x.north) -- ++(0,0.5) node[above] {$\sigma_\x$};
    }

\paragraph{Applying an operator.}  Contracting a single-site operator into an
MPS site changes the physical leg's charge by the operator's charge.  Because
the total charge is conserved, that change must propagate to the neighbouring
bonds: the bond charges ``flow'' to compensate.  The code implements this as an
ordinary contraction: the gate enters as an extra leg, \code{cached_einsum}
merges it into the site (and, for a two-site gate, into its neighbour), and the
singular-value split of Section~\ref{sec:charge-flow} puts the result back into
canonical form --- the split is what produces the new bond charges, so no
separate charge bookkeeping is needed.

\emph{Example ($S_x$ on a spin-$\tfrac12$ site).}  A spin-$\tfrac12$ physical
leg is one multiplet in sector $1$ ($\dim V_1=2$).  The spin components
$\{S_x,S_y,S_z\}$ form a triplet, sector $2$, so the operator they belong to is
an invariant rank-$3$ tensor with legs $(\mathrm{out}=1,\ \mathrm{operator}=2,
\ \mathrm{in}=1)$ fusing to trivial charge, which by \eqref{eq:we} is
$\sqrt{3/2}\,C^{121}$ (Section~\ref{sec:we}).  The code builds this multiplet
once, per sector, with \code{OperatorContent.tensor_operators}
(\path{mpsdmrg/symmetry/cat/su2}): for sector $(2,)$ it returns a tensor of
shape $(3,2,2)$ whose three slices are the spherical components, scaled so the
invariant channel satisfies $\lVert C_s\rVert^2 = \dim s = 3$.  Two slices are
the ladder matrices $\sigma_+,\sigma_-$ and the third the diagonal $\sigma_z$,
up to normalisation; the Cartesian component is the real combination
\begin{equation}
  S_x \;=\; \tfrac12\,\sigma_x \;=\; \tfrac12\,(\sigma_+ + \sigma_-)
  \;=\; \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

$S_x$ alone is one component of a charge-$2$ triplet, not a charge-definite
operator, so a bare matrix cannot act on a reduced (symmetry-adapted) leg: it
mixes the two magnetic components of the single multiplet.  For a site tensor $A$ with physical index
$p\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}$ and bond indices $\ell,r$,
\begin{equation}
  (S_x A)_{p\,\ell\,r}
  \;=\; \sum_{p'} (S_x)_{pp'}\, A_{p'\,\ell\,r}
  \;=\; \tfrac12 A_{\bar p\,\ell\,r},
\end{equation}
where $\bar p$ flips the spin, i.e.\ $(S_x A)_\uparrow=\tfrac12 A_\downarrow$
and $(S_x A)_\downarrow=\tfrac12 A_\uparrow$.  A bare matrix is therefore not an
object of a symmetric network: its entries are the magnetic components a
reduced block does not store, so the code refuses it on a state whose group has
content (\path{mpsdmrg/mps.py}) and asks for the operator as an MPO, whose bond
legs carry the charge of every channel.  An invariant channel such as $C_s$ of
\eqref{eq:channel} is an object of the network and stays on the reduced route.

\paragraph{Projection.}  Contracting an MPS and its conjugate (or an MPO into
an MPS) is a pure network contraction; the engine of Section~\ref{sec:code}
evaluates it block by block.  A projection leaves the block keys unchanged, and
its round-trip error is machine precision (Section~\ref{sec:anchors}).

\paragraph{Splitting.}  The reverse of contraction is the singular-value
decomposition that re-grows a bond: given a tensor with legs grouped into
``left'' and ``right'', split it as $T = U\, S\, V^\dagger$.  The
symmetry-aware \code{svd} proceeds in three steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Select sectors.}  Enumerate the charges the bond leg may carry ---
    exactly those that fuse the left and right legs.  Each is a candidate
    sector of the new bond.
  \item \emph{Assemble matrices.}  For each bond charge, gather the blocks that
    contribute, permute and reshape them into one dense matrix, and weight the
    left or right part by $\sqrt{\dim V_{\mathrm{charge}}}$ so that the
    singular values are measured in multiplets rather than physical
    components.
  \item \emph{Factor.}  Run a dense SVD on each matrix, collect all singular
    values, and truncate globally --- keep the largest values up to a bond
    dimension and discard the rest below a tolerance.  The surviving values,
    with their charge and copy index, assemble the new \code{bond_leg}.
\end{enumerate}

Because each charge is decomposed independently and then truncated against a
single global threshold, sectors that are dynamically irrelevant are dropped
altogether.  Finally \code{absorb} folds the singular values back into the
second factor, one value per kept multiplet, which is the unit the truncation
bound counts.

The three steps look identical for every group; only the fusion tables and the
CG tensors differ.  For an abelian group every block is $1\times1$ and the
``matrix'' per charge is scalar, for a point group (\code{z2_symmetry}) the
sectors are the group characters, and for $\SU{2}$/$\SU{3}$ the full fusion
trees of Section~\ref{sec:fusion} come into play.  This is why the group layer
exists as a single interface: the whole rest of the code is group-agnostic.

\section{Measured anchors}
\label{sec:anchors}

Each formula above is the one the engine implements; the anchors below record
the numbers that were measured for it.  The conventions are those of the code:
$\SU{2}$ sector $s=2j$, $\SU{3}$ sector $(p,q)$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{CG normalisation.}  The CG tensors satisfy
    $\lVert C^{abc}\rVert^2 = \dim V_c$.  For $\SU{2}$ the computed squared
    norms for the coupled sectors $(0),(2),(4)$ are $1$, $3$, $5$ --- exactly
    the dimensions.
  \item \emph{$\SU{3}$ dimension and Casimir.}  Formulae \eqref{eq:su3} hold
    for every sector with $p+q\le 4$.
  \item \emph{Fusion rules.}  The code's \code{fusion_outcomes} reproduces
    $8\otimes8 = 27\oplus10\oplus\overline{10}\oplus 2\cdot8\oplus1$, including
    the multiplicity $2$ of the adjoint.
  \item \emph{Block sparsity.}  A length-$16$ chain with bond dimension $64$
    stores $3806$ nonzero numbers instead of the $43688$ of the equivalent
    dense tensor, and a full sweep runs $8.5\times$ faster.
  \item \emph{Dense round trip.}  Converting \code{Tensor} $\to$ dense $\to$
    \code{Tensor} for $S^z$, $S^x$, the identity ($\SU{2}$), and $\lambda_8$
    ($\SU{3}$) reproduces the original to machine precision
    ($\max|\Delta|=0$).
  \item \emph{Charge flow.}  On an $\SU{2}$ Heisenberg chain, the norm of an
    operator of charge $1$ applied at a single site equals the direct dense
    evaluation: $0.5290063509461093$ versus $0.5290063509461105$ for $L=4$, and
    $1.1901684550722649$ versus $1.1901684550722691$ for $L=6$ with two
    operators.  The bond charge sequence grows as expected: $(1)$,
    $(0)\oplus(2)$, $(1)\oplus(3)$, $(0)\oplus(2)\oplus(4)$, $(1)$.
  \item \emph{Projection.}  Projecting a tensor onto its own sector keeps the
    block keys unchanged with error $1.2\times10^{-16}$, versus
    $3\times10^{-16}$ for the dense comparison.
  \item \emph{Absorption.}  Without repeating singular values over a multiplet,
    the sizes disagree ($4$ versus $2$) whenever a kept sector has
    $\dim V_q>1$; \code{absorb} fixes exactly this.
  \item \emph{The categorical route.}  With the Categorical weight in place,
    the table of the $22$ layouts of one $L=6$ $\SU{2}$ Heisenberg sweep is
    reproduced to $\max|\Delta| = 1.0\times10^{-15}$, and the associator of
    three spin-one legs at total $2$ is exactly \eqref{eq:F-example} with the
    braidings $R^{22}_{0}=+1$, $R^{22}_{2}=-1$, $R^{22}_{4}=+1$.
  \item \emph{Where the time goes.}  A length-$10$, bond-$32$ sweep issues
    $1.8\times10^5$ CUDA kernels averaging $2.3\,\mu$s, $2.3\times10^4$
    device-to-device copies, and $1.6\times10^5$ block slices; device time is
    $0.21\,$s against $1.4\,$s wall clock, and the $36$ SVD calls take $0.7\%$
    of the CPU.  The overhead is not the symmetry logic but the sheer number of
    small kernels --- the price of block sparsity.
\end{itemize}

The block-sparse route trades one large dense multiply for many small ones and
a lot of bookkeeping; it wins whenever the symmetry removes most of the tensor.
For $\SU{2}$ and $\SU{3}$ spin chains that is the generic case, which is why the
library is built this way.  The algorithms follow \cite{Weichselbaum_2012}; the
layered, block-addressed organisation is in the same spirit as
\cite{tenpy2024}.
