\chapter{Tensors and symmetries}
We will discuss the notions of tensors and symmetries in this chapter. We will mostly follow the conventions of \cite{tensorkit,tenpy2024}. 
\section{Linear Representations of Groups}
\begin{definition}
Let $G$ be a finite group or a compact Lie group. A \emph{linear representation} of $G$ on a vector space $V$ is a homomorphism $\rho:G\mapsto\mathsf{GL}(V)$. We will assume the representation is \emph{unitary}, i.e. $\rho(g)^\dagger=\rho(g^{-1})$ for all $g\in G$. We will also assume that the representation is \emph{finite-dimensional}, i.e. $\dim V<\infty$.
\end{definition}

It can be shown that any representation of a finite group or a compact Lie group can be decomposed into a direct sum of irreducible representations, $\rho_a$. We will use latin letters $a,b,c,\ldots$ to denote irreducible representations. We will use the notation $V^a$ \footnote{The index for different spaces are the subscripts, as we will encountered in tensor notation, whereas the irrps are the superscripts} to denote the vector space on which $\rho_a$ acts. The dimension of $V_a$ is denoted by $d_a$. The index $a$ are called sectors/charges/irreps. We will use these notations interchangeably.

\begin{example}
Let \(G=\mathbb Z_n=\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\) be the cyclic group written additively.
Identify \(\mathbb C^n\) with
\[
\mathbb C^n=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb C}\{e_m:m\in\mathbb Z_n\},
\]
where \(e_m\) is the standard basis vector indexed by \(m\in\mathbb Z_n\).
Define
\[
\rho:\mathbb Z_n\to \mathrm{GL}(\mathbb C^n),
\qquad
\rho(p)e_m:=e_{m+p},
\]
with subscripts taken modulo \(n\). This is a unitary representation: \(\rho(p)\) is a permutation matrix.

Let
\[
\zeta_n:=e^{2\pi i/n}.
\]
For each \(a\in\mathbb Z_n\), define
\[
v_a:=\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z_n}\zeta_n^{-am}e_m,
\qquad
V_a:=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb C}\{v_a\}=\mathbb C v_a.
\]
Then for every \(p\in\mathbb Z_n\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
\rho(p)v_a
&=\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z_n}\zeta_n^{-am}\rho(p)e_m
=\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z_n}\zeta_n^{-am}e_{m+p} \\
&=\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z_n}\zeta_n^{-a(j-p)}e_j
=\zeta_n^{ap}\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z_n}\zeta_n^{-aj}e_j
=\zeta_n^{ap}v_a.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus \(V^a\) is invariant under \(\rho\), and \(\rho|_{V^a}\) is the one-dimensional representation
\[
\rho_a(p)=\zeta_n^{ap}=e^{2\pi i ap/n}
\qquad (p\in\mathbb Z_n).
\]
The vectors \(\{v_a\}_{a\in\mathbb Z_n}\) form the Fourier basis of \(\mathbb C^n\), so
\[
\mathbb C^n=\bigoplus_{a\in\mathbb Z_n}V^a,
\qquad
\rho\cong\bigoplus_{a\in\mathbb Z_n}\rho_a.
\]

If one keeps the opposite sign convention, define instead
\[
v_a=\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z_n}\zeta_n^{am}e_m,
\]
in which case the eigenvalue is
\[
\zeta_n^{-ap}=e^{-2\pi i ap/n}.
\]
Physically, this is the Fourier transform, which diagonalizes the action of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ on $\mathbb{C}^n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $G = \mathsf{SU}(2)$. Its Lie algebra is given by tha angular momentum operator $\{J_x,J_y,J_z\}$ with commutation relation $[J_i,J_j]=i\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k$. Its irreducible representations $(j)$ are labeled by a non-negative half-integer $j\in\{0,1/2,1,3/2,\ldots\}$. The representation $\rho_j$ acts on a $(2j+1)$-dimensional vector space $V_j$. The representation $\rho_{1/2}$ is the fundamental representation of $\mathsf{SU}(2)$, and $\rho_1$ is the adjoint representation.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
A tensor of rank $r$ is a multilinear map \[
    T: V_{1}\times V_{2}\times\cdots\times V_{r}\to \mathbb C,
\]
where each $V_i$ is a vector space carrying a representation $\rho_i$ of a group $G$. (Not always irreducible.) By multilinear we mean that $T$ is linear in each of its arguments. Physically, these space are local Hilbert spaces or their duals.
\end{definition}

The fundamental question of our question is: if given that each vector space can be decomposed into irreducible representations $V_i = \bigoplus_{a}V_i^a$, (this part of information is given by Leg) how can we decompose the tensor $T$ into irreducible representations? To see this, first we note that $T$ it self admits a representation of $G$ given by\[
    \rho(g)T(v_1,\ldots,v_r) = T(\rho_1(g)v_1,\ldots,\rho_r(g)v_r),\quad \forall g\in G, v_i\in V_i.
\]
Or, in other words\begin{align}
    \rho(g)T = T\circ (\rho_1(g)\otimes\cdots\otimes \rho_r(g)),\quad \forall g\in G.
\end{align}
This means that $T\in (V_1\otimes \dots \otimes V_n)^*$ admits a (dual) representation of $G$ given by $\rho^*(g) = \rho_1^*(g)\otimes\cdots\otimes \rho_r^*(g)$. In general, this is still an representation of $G$, and can be decomposed into irreducible representations. In general, we may argue that the tensor $T$ it self lies in (or at least, can be decomposed into) the irreducible representation of $G$ with sector $a$, and call it $T$ have charge $a$ for short. Usually we also require $a=0$ the trivial representation. In this case, we say that $T$ is a \emph{invariant tensor}. 

This brings several important benefits. If $T$ is an invariant tensor\footnote{or in general, a tensor with charge $a$, as we will see the following arguments will spiritually the same}, then the tensor $T$ can be reduced in a block diagonal form, as certain subspace may not fuse to the trivial sector.  

\begin{example}
Let $G=\mathbb{Z}_2$, the irrps are labeled by $a\in\{0,1\}$, and the representation $\rho_a$ acts on a one-dimensional vector space $V^a$. The tensor product of two irreps is given by $\rho_a\otimes \rho_b = \rho_{(a+b)\mod 2}$. Thus, for a rank-2 tensor $T\in (V_1\otimes V_2)^*$ to be invariant, we must have $V_1^a\otimes V_2^b$ such that $(a+b)\mod 2=0$, i.e. $b=a$. This means that the invariant tensor can only connect sectors with the same charge. To be more concrete, let $v_1\in V_1^{a}$ and $v_2\in V_2^{b}$, then the invariant tensor $T$ satisfies
\begin{align}
    T(v_1,v_2) = T(\rho_a(g)v_1,\rho_b(g)v_2) = T((-1)^{ag}v_1,(-1)^{bg}v_2) = (-1)^{(a+b)g}T(v_1,v_2),\quad \forall g\in \mathbb{Z}_2.
\end{align}
This implies that $T(v_1,v_2)=0$ unless $(a+b)\mod 2=0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $G=\mathsf{U}(1)$, the irrps are labeled by integers $a\in\mathbb{Z}$, and the representation $\rho_a$ acts on a one-dimensional vector space $V^a$. The tensor product of two irreps is given by $\rho_a\otimes \rho_b = \rho_{a+b}$. Thus, for a rank-2 tensor $T\in (V_1\otimes V_2)^*$ to be invariant, we must have $V_1^a\otimes V_2^b$ such that $a+b=0$, i.e. $b=-a$. This means that the invariant tensor can only connect sectors with opposite charges. This is simply the charge conservation law. To be more concrete, let $v_1\in V_1^{a}$ and $v_2\in V_2^{b}$, then the invariant tensor $T$ satisfies
\begin{align}
    T(v_1,v_2) = T(\rho_a(g)v_1,\rho_b(g)v_2) = T(e^{iag}v_1,e^{ibg}v_2) = e^{i(a+b)g}T(v_1,v_2),\quad \forall g\in \mathsf{U}(1).
\end{align}
This implies that $T(v_1,v_2)=0$ unless $a+b=0$.
\end{example}


The previous two examples are simple as the group $G$ is abelian, whose irreps must be one-dimensional. For non-abelian groups, the irrps can be higher-dimensional, and the tensor product of two irrps can decompose into a direct sum of multiple irrps. 

\begin{example}
Let $G=\mathsf{SU}(2)$ and $T\in (V_1\otimes V_2)^*$ be a rank-2 invariant tensor, where\begin{align}
    V_1 = (0)\oplus(1)\oplus(2)\oplus(3),\quad V_2 = (0)\oplus(1)\oplus(2).
\end{align}Tensor product of them distributes to\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
        $V_2 \backslash V_1$& $V_1^{(0)}$ & $V_1^{(1)}$ & $V_1^{(2)}$ & $V_1^{(3)}$ \\
        \hline
        $V_2^{(0)}$ & $(0)$ & $(1)$ & $(2)$ & $(3)$ \\
        $V_2^{(1)}$ & $(1)$ & $(0)\oplus(1)\oplus(2)$ & $(1)\oplus(2)\oplus(3)$ & $(2)\oplus(3)$ \\
        $V_2^{(2)}$ & $(2)$ & $(1)\oplus(2)\oplus(3)$ & $(0)\oplus(1)\oplus(2)\oplus(3)\oplus(4)$ & $(1)\oplus(2)\oplus(3)$ 
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}
We see that, for the tensor $T$ to be invariant, we only need to consider tensor on these subspaces: $V_1^{(0)}\otimes V_2^{(0)}$, $V_1^{(1)}\otimes V_2^{(1)}$, $V_1^{(2)}\otimes V_2^{(2)}$. The other subspaces do not contain the trivial representation, and thus cannot contribute to the invariant tensor. (in a matrix language, it only carries the map $V_1^{(0)}\to V_2^{(0)}$, $V_1^{(1)}\to V_2^{(1)}$, $V_1^{(2)}\to V_2^{(2)}$ and the whole tensor as a direct sum of these).
\end{example}
Note that the matrix is now block diagonal, which is essentially the Schur's lemma. 
\section{Fusion trees}
\label{sec:tree}In general, for a tensor with rank $r>2$ we therefore have to say in which order the irreps are fused. 
A \emph{fusion tree} records the order in which the legs are fused. It is a full binary tree whose $r$ leaves are the legs, whose $r-1$ inner vertices each carry a charge $b_v$, and whose root carries the charge $a$ of the tensor. Every inner vertex is the constraint
\begin{align}
    b_v \in b_{v_L}\otimes b_{v_R},
\end{align}
where $b_{v_L},b_{v_R}$ are the charges of its two children and a leaf carries the charge of its leg. The tree therefore attaches $r-1$ intermediate charges to the tensor, on top of the $r$ leg charges. The number of shapes with $r$ leaves is the Catalan number $C_{r-1}$: two for $r=3$, five for $r=4$.

\begin{example}
The leaves alone do not fix the tree. Let $G=\mathsf{SU}(2)$ and let all four legs be spin-$\tfrac12$,
\begin{align}
    T\in (\tfrac12)\otimes(\tfrac12)\otimes(\tfrac12)\otimes(\tfrac12),
\end{align}
with $T$ invariant. The leaves, the leg charges and the total charge are the same in every tree, and so is the number of blocks: four spin-$\tfrac12$ couple to $0$ in two ways, so the invariant space is two-dimensional and $T$ has two blocks in any shape. What the tree changes is the pair of inner charges that labels those two blocks.
\begin{center}
    \fusiontreeivladder{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac12$}{$b_1$}{$b_2$}
    \qquad
    \fusiontreeivbalanced{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac12$}{$c_1$}{$c_2$}
\end{center}
In the left-nested tree the first vertex couples the first two legs, $b_1\in\tfrac12\otimes\tfrac12=0\oplus 1$, and whatever $b_1$ is, the coupling with the third leg must leave $b_2=\tfrac12$ for the last fusion to reach $0$: the two blocks are $(b_1,b_2)=(0,\tfrac12)$ and $(1,\tfrac12)$. In the balanced tree the two pairs couple separately, $c_1,c_2\in 0\oplus 1$, and the root reaches $0$ only when $c_1=c_2$: the two blocks are $(c_1,c_2)=(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. Same leaves, same number of blocks, different labels --- a block is an inner charge as much as it is a set of leg charges.
\end{example}



A block is fixed by a charge on each leg. It is nonzero only when the tree can be labelled consistently with those leg charges and with $a$, so a block is labelled by the pair (leg charges, inner charges). Several inner labellings can share one set of leg charges, and the tensor then has several blocks there, one per labelling.

\begin{example}
Let $G=\mathsf{SU}(2)$ and consider an invariant rank-3 tensor $T\in V_1\otimes V_2\otimes V_3$ with
\begin{align}
    V_1 = 7\cdot(0)\oplus 5\cdot(\tfrac12)\oplus 3\cdot(1)\oplus 1\cdot(\tfrac32),\qquad
    V_2 = (\tfrac12),\qquad
    V_3 = V_1^*.
\end{align}
The bond spaces have dimension $7+5\cdot 2+3\cdot 3+1\cdot 4=30$, so a dense array for $T$ has
\begin{align}
    30\times 2\times 30 = 1800 \text{ entries},\qquad 1800\times 16\ \mathrm{B} = 28.8\ \mathrm{kB}
\end{align}
in complex double precision. Of the $4\times 1\times 4=16$ combinations of leg charges only six can fuse to the trivial charge $a=0$; those six are
\begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{m{2.6cm}cc}
        tree & vector entries & stored numbers \\
        \hline
        \fusiontreepic{$0$}{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac12$} & $7\cdot 2\cdot 10 = 140$ & $7\cdot 1\cdot 5 = 35$ \\[2em]
        \fusiontreepic{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac12$}{$0$}{$0$} & $10\cdot 2\cdot 7 = 140$ & $5\cdot 1\cdot 7 = 35$ \\[2em]
        \fusiontreepic{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac12$}{$1$}{$1$} & $10\cdot 2\cdot 9 = 180$ & $5\cdot 1\cdot 3 = 15$ \\[2em]
        \fusiontreepic{$1$}{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac12$} & $9\cdot 2\cdot 10 = 180$ & $3\cdot 1\cdot 5 = 15$ \\[2em]
        \fusiontreepic{$1$}{$\tfrac12$}{$\tfrac32$}{$\tfrac32$} & $9\cdot 2\cdot 4 = 72$ & $3\cdot 1\cdot 1 = 3$ \\[2em]
        \fusiontreepic{$\tfrac32$}{$\tfrac12$}{$1$}{$1$} & $4\cdot 2\cdot 9 = 72$ & $1\cdot 1\cdot 3 = 3$ \\
        \hline
        total & $784$ & $106$
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}
Each row of the table is one block, drawn in the first column as a fusion tree: it fuses $V_1$ with $V_2$ first, carries the inner charge $b$ on the edge between the two vertices, and fuses the result with $V_3$ to the trivial charge $0$ at the root, while its leaves are labelled by the leg charges $a_1,a_2,a_3$.

The two number columns count different things. The second is the size of the block if it were stored densely, the product of its three leg blocks; the third is the number of numbers the engine actually stores, one per triple of multiplets. The factor between them is $d_{a_1}d_{a_2}d_{a_3}$, the number of components each multiplet carries, which the Clebsch--Gordan coefficients of $b=a_1\otimes a_2$ rebuild from that single number.

The symmetry thus compresses the tensor twice over. Sparsity removes the ten forbidden leg-charge combinations, $1800\to 784$; the multiplet structure then removes the Clebsch--Gordan degeneracy, $784\to 106$. The result is $106\times 16\ \mathrm{B}=1.7\ \mathrm{kB}$, a factor $17$ below the dense array. The factor does not improve as the tensor grows: multiplying every multiplicity by $k$ multiplies both counts by $k^2$ and leaves the ratio at $1/17$. The saving is a fixed overhead of the group, not an exponential one.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Now add a second physical leg, $T\in V_1\otimes V_2\otimes V_2'\otimes V_3$ with the same $V_1,V_3$ and two spin-$\tfrac12$ legs. The dense array doubles to $30\cdot 2\cdot 2\cdot 30=3600$ entries, $57.6\ \mathrm{kB}$. Of the $4\cdot 1\cdot 1\cdot 4=16$ leg-charge combinations, eight admit a labelling, and three of those admit two, so there are $8+3=11$ blocks storing $171$ numbers, $2.74\ \mathrm{kB}$.

The doubling is where the tree enters the numbers. Take the leg charges $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ and fuse left to right. The first vertex is $\tfrac12\otimes\tfrac12=0\oplus 1$, and both choices fuse with the second spin-$\tfrac12$ to $\tfrac12$, which fuses with the last leg to $0$. Two inner charges, $b_1=0$ and $b_1=1$, both consistent: two blocks, each weighted by one Clebsch--Gordan coefficient. Every block that doubles is a pair of this kind, and which leg-charge combinations double depends on the order: with the two physical legs adjacent the doubled triples are $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, $(1,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,1)$, $(\tfrac32,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac32)$; with them separated they are $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, $(1,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac12)$, $(\tfrac32,\tfrac12,\tfrac32,\tfrac12)$.

What the tree does not change is how much is stored. The engine keeps one shape, the left-nested chain, so the only freedom is which leg sits at which position, and permuting the legs permutes the labels: every order gives eleven blocks and $171$ numbers. The tree decides which intermediate charge a block carries, and that charge decides which Clebsch--Gordan coefficient weights it; the two blocks that share a set of leg charges are exactly the pair an associator mixes when the tree is changed, which is what makes that mixing well defined.

Where the tree does change the cost is in a contraction, because the intermediate charge sets the dimension of the bond that is summed over. Fusing the bond leg with a spin-$\tfrac12$ leg gives a bond of dimension $60$, fusing the two spin-$\tfrac12$ legs gives $4$, and fusing the two bond legs gives $900$. The same contraction therefore costs very differently depending on where the tree puts its vertices, and choosing the tree is choosing the order of the sums.
\end{example}
